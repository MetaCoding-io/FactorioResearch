# The Province — World Design

**Status:** compact worldbuilding constraint document. This is intentionally not a lore bible.

**Purpose:** establish enough of the fictional world's technological, institutional, ecological, and visual grammar to make scenes coherent. This document answers questions such as: What can break? What can the Coordinator plausibly know? What does a railway, clinic, power station, planning office, or network operations room look like? What kinds of institutions can act? It should constrain the story without requiring the reader to study the world before the story begins.

The narrative design remains canonical in [`NARRATIVE_DESIGN_BIBLE.md`](NARRATIVE_DESIGN_BIBLE.md). This document specializes its setting decisions.

---

## 1. World position

### Decided: parallel-present / near-present industrial society

The story does **not** take place in literal 1970s Chile, the Soviet Union, or an alternate historical Cybersyn timeline. It takes place in a secondary or parallel world whose technical capabilities are broadly recognizable as modern.

The world has:

- packet-switched telecommunications and fiber networks;
- industrial Ethernet, radio links, telemetry, remote terminal units, and distributed control systems;
- computerized railway signaling and dispatch;
- modern electrical-grid monitoring and automation;
- software-defined planning and simulation tools;
- renewable generation alongside older hydro, thermal, and other legacy plant;
- capable generative software that can implement code from specifications;
- ordinary consumer computing and communications, without requiring the story to imitate any specific 2020s product interface.

Technology is **uneven**, as real infrastructure is uneven. A new fiber-connected control center may supervise equipment installed forty years earlier. A modern train-control system may cross a microwave backhaul link nobody has replaced because it still works. A sophisticated generative coding system may run on institutional computing infrastructure whose physical hardware looks old. Paper, radio, terminals, handwritten field notes, and modern software comfortably coexist.

This unevenness is a feature of the setting. It creates the boundaries where interesting failures live.

### Why this matters

The Coordinator can come from a real infrastructure-debugging tradition: networking, telecommunications, control systems, railway signaling, industrial automation, and power telemetry. Their formative victories can involve failures such as MTU mismatches, clock drift, corrupt gateways, asymmetric network paths, bad firmware, exhausted tables, faulty memory, or legacy equipment interacting unexpectedly with newer systems.

The world therefore gives the Coordinator a strong and reasonable initial belief:

> Large failures often have small causes. Find the broken thing.

The novel later complicates that belief without making it foolish.

## 2. Political and economic texture

### Decided: a mixed but strongly socialized infrastructure economy

The world should not be a disguised argument about whether the historical Soviet model succeeded or failed. Nor should it simply reproduce a contemporary capitalist economy with different scenery.

The society has made different institutional choices from ours. Large infrastructure and essential services are commonly organized through some mixture of:

- public utilities;
- municipal or provincial enterprises;
- worker or producer cooperatives;
- national infrastructure agencies;
- planning bodies that negotiate material commitments and long-horizon investment;
- markets for many ordinary goods and services;
- local autonomy inside broader public obligations.

The Province therefore receives and negotiates **commitments**, not merely financial targets. It may owe power, freight capacity, machinery, agricultural products, reserve capability, or other services to the larger system while also guaranteeing local services.

Money exists. Prices exist. Budgets exist. But the story's operational questions are deliberately expressed first in physical quantities: megawatts, train paths, tons, machine-hours, water, emissions, reserve margin, delivery windows, and service floors. Financial accounting does not magically substitute for understanding the physical system.

### Decided: the national planning system is not the villain

The Committee Liaison and higher-level institutions should generally contain competent people confronting the same class of problems at a larger recursion.

The center sometimes makes bad decisions. Provinces sometimes make bad decisions. Information can be delayed, incentives can distort reports, and institutions can protect obsolete practices. But the narrative should resist a simple center-bad/local-good or state-bad/market-good axis.

The central problem is **how autonomy and cohesion can coexist**.

### Strong lean: the Province is not politically isolated

People can travel, communicate, disagree publicly, read outside technical work, and know that other institutional arrangements exist. The society need not be culturally closed in order to have a serious planning tradition.

This helps the book remain about organizational cybernetics rather than about life under an authoritarian state.

## 3. The Province

### Decided: remote enough to matter, connected enough to be modern

The Province is geographically distant from the country's principal industrial and administrative centers. Distance makes transport, maintenance, inventory, communications resilience, and external rescue materially consequential.

It is not a frontier with no infrastructure. It already contains towns, agriculture, industry, power systems, rail, communications networks, public institutions, and people with long histories there.

The new delegation is therefore **not arriving to civilize an empty map**. It is entering an existing social and technical system whose local knowledge predates them.

That is important for the Veteran Operator, the governed characters, and the book's attitude toward expertise.

### Strong lean: major provincial systems

The Province should plausibly contain enough interdependence to support the whole curriculum:

- an electrified freight and passenger rail network;
- generation, transmission, storage, and distribution infrastructure;
- agricultural districts and food processing;
- workshops and light/heavy industrial production;
- mining or mineral extraction sufficient to make depletion and development real;
- a clinic or hospital system whose service requirements put a human face on infrastructure metrics;
- water and watershed systems;
- telecom and industrial data networks;
- one or more universities, technical institutes, or engineering centers;
- provincial planning and operations institutions.

The exact geography remains open until plot needs make it concrete.

## 4. Ecological commitments

### Decided: ecological limits are normal governance, not decorative virtue

This is one of the setting's strongest solarpunk-adjacent commitments.

Environmental constraints are not presented as an external nuisance imposed on the productive system. They are part of what the society understands its productive system to be *for*.

The Province may operate under explicit limits such as:

- annual or seasonal emissions ceilings;
- minimum river flow;
- watershed nutrient or contaminant limits;
- water-withdrawal ceilings;
- soil-restoration or land-use requirements;
- air-quality limits;
- reserve requirements for extreme heat or cold;
- habitat or forestry constraints.

These are physical constraints and should be measurable whenever the story depends on them.

### Decided: ecological commitments may conflict with other genuine goods

The Ecologist should not exist merely to say no, and environmental rules should not always point toward an easy moral answer.

A winter emergency might make an old thermal plant attractive precisely when doing so threatens an annual emissions ceiling. Increased irrigation might save a harvest while violating river-flow requirements. Rapid mine development might protect future industrial viability while imposing local ecological cost.

The interesting question is not whether ecology matters. That is settled in this society.

The question is how a viable system maintains several commitments that become difficult to satisfy simultaneously.

This gives S5 real material to arbitrate.

## 5. Solarpunk adjacency

### Decided: solarpunk is an influence, not a genre contract

The setting borrows several solarpunk commitments:

- belief that better institutions can be designed;
- ecological boundaries treated seriously;
- public and cooperative infrastructure;
- repair, maintenance, adaptation, and reuse;
- dense human settlements connected by shared transport;
- renewable and distributed energy;
- technological sophistication without technological salvation;
- a generally hopeful belief in collective competence.

But the Province is **not already utopian**.

Renewables do not abolish grid regulation. Public ownership does not abolish information problems. Cooperation does not abolish conflict. Planning does not create omniscience. AI does not remove specification errors. Ecological commitments do not remove scarcity. Competent people can still inhabit badly designed relationships.

The story's optimism comes from the possibility of learning and redesign, not from pretending hard systems problems disappeared.

### Tone consequence

The world should contain ordinary pleasures and visible evidence that people have successfully built things together.

Rail stations can be pleasant civic infrastructure. Public gardens can coexist with substations. Technical people can enjoy their work. Maintenance can be respected. People can argue fiercely and remain colleagues. A public utility can be something citizens are proud of even when it fails them.

This matters because the reader should understand why the characters think their institutions are worth improving.

## 6. Visual and material language

### Strong lean: industrial solarpunk rather than ornamental solarpunk

The Province should not default to glass towers covered in decorative vines.

Its visual identity is more likely to come from:

- electrified rail corridors;
- catenary, substations, switchyards, and signal gantries;
- hydro works, wind fields, solar installations, storage facilities, and older generating plant;
- brick, concrete, timber, weathered steel, repaired machinery, and retrofitted buildings;
- compact towns organized around rail and public space;
- machine halls next to wetlands or agricultural land;
- control rooms where old panels coexist with newer displays;
- field cabinets, fiber trays, radios, test equipment, patch panels, terminals, notebooks, and hand-labelled repairs;
- greenhouses, grain elevators, workshops, clinics, tram lines, depots, and public technical institutes.

The aesthetic should communicate **layers of accumulated infrastructure** rather than one clean technological era.

Maintenance scars are part of the beauty.

### Strong lean: the Ops Room is visually impressive but never magical

It may deliberately rhyme with Cybersyn: shared displays, spatial presentation of operational state, exception-oriented information, and a room designed for people to think together.

But it remains a human decision environment, not the mind of the Province.

Its eventual danger comes precisely when better visibility tempts the Coordinator to confuse seeing with controlling.

## 7. Factorio and the generative machine

### Decided: Factorio is diegetic, subject to later naming/licensing review

Within the current creative design, the Coordinator genuinely uses **Factorio** as a modeling environment. The Province is not a Factorio world; Factorio is an abstraction the Coordinator uses to represent parts of the Province.

A grain elevator may become chests and flows. A workshop may become assemblers. A transport problem may become belts or trains. The abstraction gap is intentional and becomes part of the story's epistemology.

The narrative should be written so that the game could be renamed later without changing any plot logic if a future licensing or partnership decision requires it.

### Decided: capable generative software exists

The Coordinator has access to an institutional generative coding system capable of turning specifications into working software quickly enough to make iterative model-building practical.

The story does not need to explain contemporary model architectures, vendors, chat interfaces, token counts, or product-specific behavior.

The machine's dramatic function is stable:

- fast builder;
- literal implementer;
- powerful amplifier;
- no independent governance role;
- improves nothing it was not asked to improve;
- its most meaningful failures expose missing or mistaken specifications.

It belongs in the **workshop**, not the Ops Room.

### Strong lean: the machine's physical host has history

The generative system may run on or through institutional computing infrastructure connected to the Old Cyberneticist's earlier national planning/computation project.

This preserves the historical rhyme without making the story take place in the 1970s: the old dream of central visibility leaves behind tools that are eventually used for a more distributed philosophy of learning.

Details remain open.

## 8. Communications and infrastructure failures

### Decided: modern networking failures are native story material

The Coordinator's professional world should plausibly contain failures familiar to real infrastructure engineering:

- MTU and fragmentation mismatches;
- clock or time-synchronization drift;
- packet corruption or rare bit errors;
- asymmetric routes;
- partial failures that evade ordinary health checks;
- legacy/new protocol boundaries;
- firmware defects;
- buffer exhaustion;
- stale state;
- incorrect failover assumptions;
- radio interference;
- overloaded links;
- bad monitoring that reports green while service is failing;
- physically healthy components participating in a collectively unhealthy system.

These details should be used selectively. Technical authenticity is valuable; gratuitous jargon is not.

The opening war story is likely to involve a modern rail/communications failure where a very small defect causes a large operational disturbance. The Coordinator solves it correctly, reinforcing the worldview that later becomes insufficient.

## 9. What the world should *not* become

Avoid:

- a generic dystopian command economy;
- a frictionless green utopia;
- a cyberpunk world with a green paint job;
- an AI-governed society;
- a setting where every institution is stupid except the protagonist;
- a direct fictionalization of the historical USSR or Chile;
- technology that exists only to solve a plot problem;
- worldbuilding exposition that arrives before a character needs the fact;
- treating ecological limits as moral decoration rather than operational constraints;
- treating public planning as either inherently omniscient or inherently absurd.

The world exists to make the story's structural questions visible.

## 10. Open decisions

These should remain unfixed until the story needs them:

1. **Country and province names.** Secondary-world naming scheme is open.
2. **Exact calendar year.** The setting may never need to state one.
3. **Geography and climate.** Winter currently has useful dramatic weight, but exact latitude, terrain, coast/inland status, and resources are open.
4. **Governmental constitution.** We know the texture better than the formal constitutional system; do not over-design it early.
5. **Degree of market activity.** Mixed economy is decided; the exact boundary between planned commitments, cooperative exchange, and market allocation is open.
6. **The Old Cyberneticist's failed project.** Historical inspiration and emotional function are clearer than its fictional details.
7. **The generative machine's physical form and history.** Function is decided; hardware mythology is not.
8. **Factorio naming/licensing.** Factorio is the working diegetic choice. Keep the manuscript structurally renameable until any external relationship is settled.
9. **Architecture and visual references.** Industrial solarpunk is the direction; specific regional influences can emerge once locations and characters acquire names.

## 11. Scene test

When worldbuilding enters a scene, it should usually answer at least one of these questions:

- What does this character want?
- What can physically fail here?
- What information can this character actually see?
- What institution has authority to act?
- What resource or ecological boundary constrains the choice?
- What does the measured quantity mean to a person who depends on it?
- What assumption about the world is the Coordinator currently making?

If a world detail answers none of them, it probably belongs in notes rather than the novel.

The standing rule is:

> **Build only enough world to make the next human and technical decision real.**

# The Province — World Design

**Status:** compact worldbuilding constraint document. This is intentionally not a lore bible.

**Purpose:** establish enough of the fictional world's technological, institutional, ecological, and material grammar to make scenes coherent. This document answers questions such as: Why can an advanced railway still fail? Why does the country need the Province to work? What knowledge disappeared at independence? What can the Coordinator plausibly know? What authority does a worker council, province, or national body possess? It should constrain the story without requiring the reader to study the world before the story begins.

The narrative design remains canonical in [`NARRATIVE_DESIGN_BIBLE.md`](NARRATIVE_DESIGN_BIBLE.md). This document specializes its setting decisions.

---

## 1. Foundational premise

### Decided: political independence has arrived before institutional viability

The story takes place in a **newly independent country** that has inherited a surprisingly advanced industrial and technical base from a larger political and economic system.

The new country did not begin from technological backwardness. It inherited:

- electrified rail and computerized signaling;
- hydroelectric and thermal generation, plus newer renewable plant;
- mines, workshops, agricultural complexes, and large industrial facilities;
- fiber, radio, industrial networks, telemetry, and distributed control systems;
- universities and technical institutes;
- public-sector computing infrastructure and sophisticated software;
- experienced technicians, operators, engineers, and workers who know their facilities intimately.

But it did **not** inherit the complete system that made those assets function together.

Critical dependencies once lived outside the new country's borders or above its institutions:

- spare-parts supply chains;
- locomotive, turbine, and industrial-equipment vendors;
- specialist engineering teams;
- proprietary firmware and support contracts;
- centralized dispatch and planning functions;
- cross-border production chains;
- senior managerial and administrative layers;
- access to imported fuels, chemicals, lubricants, electronics, and machine tools;
- tacit knowledge that was never written down because the old system did not need to write it down.

The country therefore inherits **machinery without the full organization that made the machinery viable**.

That is the setting's central asymmetry:

> **Technical inheritance is not institutional capability.**

The country has achieved political independence. The story asks whether it can learn how to remain independent operationally.

## 2. Independence and political texture

### Decided: the new order is decentralized, participatory, and suspicious of recreated hierarchy

The independence movement was not fought merely to replace one national administration with another identical command structure.

The new political settlement strongly values:

- provincial and municipal autonomy;
- worker self-management;
- cooperatives and worker councils;
- participatory local government;
- federations and negotiated commitments rather than simple administrative command;
- ecological stewardship as part of self-government;
- political pluralism inside a shared independence project.

Rojava / democratic-confederalist politics, worker-recovered enterprises, cooperative traditions, and other experiments in decentralized self-government are **creative inspirations**, not settings to reproduce literally.

A recurring political instinct is:

> **We did not win self-government in order to rebuild the old hierarchy under a new flag.**

This makes the organizational problem real. Centralizing every difficult decision may be technically tempting but politically contrary to the founding project. Letting every workplace or province act entirely independently is equally incapable of sustaining a national railway, grid, food system, or industrial strategy.

The country's great unresolved question is therefore already VSM-shaped:

> **How can autonomous units remain genuinely autonomous while still acting coherently together?**

The novel should discover the answer through events rather than state the question this cleanly in dialogue.

### Decided: the national institutions are young, not stupid

The national council, ministries/agencies, and planning bodies contain competent people trying to invent institutions while operating them.

They have little inherited legitimacy, incomplete information, uncertain jurisdiction, and sometimes contradictory obligations. Rules are still being written. Some responsibilities that once belonged to the former center have no obvious new owner.

The Committee Liaison is therefore not a representative of a monolithic old bureaucracy. They are someone facing the same problem as the Province **one recursion upward**.

The narrative should resist simple binaries:

- center bad / locality good;
- state bad / market good;
- experts bad / workers good;
- old knowledge bad / revolutionary improvisation good.

Every level possesses knowledge the others need.

## 3. Worker takeovers and inherited industry

### Strong lean: major facilities survived independence because their workers kept them alive

During the transition, former owners, parent-state administrators, foreign managers, contractors, or corporate headquarters withdraw from some facilities. A number are ordered closed or simply abandoned.

Workers refuse to let all of them die.

Possible examples include:

- a rolling-stock workshop reorganized as a worker cooperative;
- a fertilizer or food-processing plant kept running by its operators;
- a mine whose local crews assume operational control;
- a generating station whose staff establish an emergency management council;
- logistics depots and repair shops whose workers begin coordinating directly with one another.

These takeovers should be neither romanticized nor mocked.

A turbine crew that has maintained the same machines for twenty years may know more about the plant than any departed executive. But running the plant is not identical to deciding:

- how much reserve capacity the country needs;
- when to sacrifice current output for overhaul;
- how to replace equipment whose vendor has left;
- how one enterprise's decisions affect the grid, railway, agriculture, or neighboring provinces;
- how to allocate scarce capital and imported components;
- how to coordinate across hundreds of autonomous units.

Local operational competence can be excellent while metasystem capability remains immature.

This is one of the story's most important sources of dignity: the people are not waiting to be saved by the Coordinator. They have already saved a great deal themselves.

## 4. The external pressure

### Strong lean: independence is followed by a severe oil / industrial-import embargo or equivalent trade rupture

The structural problem created by independence is given urgency by an external economic shock.

A former dominant state, neighboring bloc, consortium of former owners, or hostile trading coalition restricts access to some combination of:

- petroleum and refined fuels;
- lubricants;
- industrial electronics;
- machine tools;
- turbine and locomotive components;
- specialty chemicals;
- proprietary software support;
- foreign engineering services.

The exact geopolitical cause remains open. It may involve non-recognition of the new state, expropriation disputes, strategic pressure, or a broader regional crisis.

The important narrative function is decided:

> **Independence creates the organizational problem; the embargo creates the clock.**

The country cannot simply keep operating inherited systems as before.

Electrified rail, renewable generation, local repair capacity, recycling, remanufacturing, alternative agricultural inputs, demand management, and domestic manufacturing become matters of national survival rather than aesthetic preference.

This is where the setting's solarpunk direction comes from.

## 5. Solarpunk as destination, not backdrop

### Decided: solarpunk is what the country is trying to build

The country is **not already a polished ecological utopia**.

It has political commitments that point toward a solarpunk future:

- democratic control of essential infrastructure;
- social ecology;
- repair and reuse;
- electrification;
- public transport;
- distributed and renewable energy;
- cooperative production;
- ecological ceilings treated as real limits;
- confidence that institutions can be redesigned rather than merely endured.

But the material reality is difficult.

There are aging thermal plants beside new wind installations. Trains are electric but replacement power electronics are scarce. A solar field may be modern while its communications backhaul crosses obsolete equipment. A worker-run factory may have excellent craft knowledge and no reliable source for bearings. A public utility may have ambitious ecological obligations and inadequate reserve capacity.

The visual and emotional movement of the story is therefore not **arrival in the green future**, but **people building pieces of it under pressure**.

The optimism comes from learning, solidarity, maintenance, and institutional invention—not technological abundance.

## 6. The Province

### Decided: one region matters disproportionately to the independence project

The Province is one of the new country's major industrial/agricultural regions and contains enough interdependence to make its success nationally important.

It likely includes some combination of:

- an electrified freight and passenger rail corridor;
- hydroelectric generation;
- older thermal generation that remains difficult to retire;
- newer wind/solar/storage projects;
- agricultural districts and food processing;
- mining or mineral extraction;
- rolling-stock, machine, or electrical workshops;
- inherited industrial plants whose former owners have withdrawn;
- a clinic or hospital network;
- water and watershed systems;
- telecom and industrial data networks;
- technical institutes or engineering schools.

The Province is not empty territory waiting for a delegation to organize it. It contains communities, institutions, workers, and local political structures that predate the Coordinator's arrival.

The delegation enters an existing system whose knowledge is distributed among people who often understand their own environments better than the newcomers do.

### Decided: rescue is expensive because the country itself is fragile

Early in the story, the Province appears to meet many of its commitments because the national system repeatedly rescues it:

- emergency fuel;
- imported spare parts purchased with scarce foreign exchange;
- diverted grain;
- engineering teams pulled from other regions;
- replacement equipment borrowed from strategic reserves;
- temporary electricity or transport capacity reassigned from elsewhere.

Every rescue has an opportunity cost somewhere else in the new state.

The later rescue-ledger revelation therefore carries national moral weight. The Province has not merely been inefficient. It has been consuming the independence project's scarce emergency capacity while reporting apparent success.

The eventual line:

> **EXTERNAL RESCUE: 0**

means more than local operational success. Political independence has begun to acquire material depth.

## 7. Technology: advanced, uneven, and orphaned

### Decided: modern technical capability exists, but maintenance ecosystems have fractured

The setting supports:

- packet-switched networking and fiber;
- industrial Ethernet and fieldbus systems;
- radio and microwave backhaul;
- computerized railway signaling and dispatch;
- modern grid telemetry and automation;
- distributed control systems;
- sophisticated simulation and planning software;
- capable local computation;
- generative software that can implement code from specifications.

But none of this technology is magic or self-maintaining.

A network controller can work perfectly while the vendor who understands one opaque protocol is now across a hostile border. The country's best locomotive fleet can depend on a particular imported inverter. A modern control center may supervise equipment built in four different political eras.

Technology is layered, repaired, reverse-engineered, repurposed, and sometimes run far beyond its intended support life.

This creates authentic failure boundaries for the Coordinator:

- MTU mismatches;
- clock drift;
- corrupt gateways;
- asymmetric routing;
- partial failures that evade health checks;
- firmware defects;
- obsolete interfaces;
- bad failover assumptions;
- proprietary black boxes;
- missing documentation;
- degraded hardware that fails only under particular conditions.

The Coordinator's formative worldview therefore remains reasonable:

> **Large failures often have small causes. Find the broken thing.**

The Province will eventually confront them with failures where every component can be functioning and the relationship among the components is what is broken.

## 8. The Old Cyberneticist and the inherited planning system

### Decided: modeling is common; his unusual knowledge is about the limits of central modeling

The Old Cyberneticist should **not** be the only person in an advanced society who understands simulation, feedback, or systems analysis.

Grid engineers model loads. Railway planners simulate traffic. Agronomists forecast harvests. Factories model processes. Government agencies run planning models.

His scar comes from a different place.

Before independence, he participated in a prestigious national systems / planning-computation project whose ambition was to make the old center capable of seeing and coordinating an enormous economy through better information and better models.

He once believed that vision.

The project may have produced impressive dashboards, forecasts, optimization systems, and control rooms. Yet better central visibility increasingly became confused with better central control. Local autonomy narrowed. Reporting adapted to what headquarters wanted to see. Decision latency could rise even while information quality improved.

The project eventually failed politically, institutionally, or operationally and became associated with the old system's hubris.

This is the Old Cyberneticist's wound.

He does not possess a magical forgotten solution. He understands why **more accurate representation at the center does not eliminate the organizational problem**.

Independence gives that unfinished problem a second life under radically different political conditions:

- the old system had too much cohesion and too little autonomy;
- the young country risks autonomy without enough cohesion.

The book searches for the third thing.

## 9. Factorio, FISL, and the generative machine

### Decided: Factorio is a real game in the fictional world, subject to later naming/licensing review

Factorio is not a simulation of the fictional territory itself. It is a deliberately simplified modeling medium used by the Coordinator.

The Coordinator may choose it partly because official planning software carries assumptions, abstractions, or institutional baggage inherited from the old system. Factorio offers a transparent little world in which flows, queues, capacities, and constraints can be constructed and inspected directly.

The game begins as a practical modeling toy and becomes inadequate one problem at a time. FISL accretes from those inadequacies.

The manuscript should remain structurally renameable if a future Wube/licensing relationship requires it.

### Strong lean: the generative machine is inherited strategic infrastructure

The old national systems institute or another technical institution possessed locally operated foundation models / generative coding systems before independence.

Foreign cloud access, vendor support, or continual model updates may disappear after the political rupture, but the country still possesses:

- the model weights;
- local compute;
- enough engineering knowledge to keep the system running;
- a capable, slightly dated generative coding tool.

This helps explain why the machine is simultaneously powerful and constrained.

Its function remains:

- fast builder;
- literal implementer;
- powerful amplifier;
- no independent governance role;
- improves nothing it was not asked to improve;
- exposes specification failures rather than supplying political wisdom.

The machine belongs in the **workshop, not the Ops Room**.

There is a deliberate historical rhyme: infrastructure once intended to increase the old center's ability to model and control the country is repurposed to help people build smaller, falsifiable models that support distributed learning.

## 10. Ecological commitments

### Decided: ecological limits are constitutional commitments of the new society

Social ecology is not decorative worldbuilding. The independence project includes a belief that self-government also means responsibility for the material conditions that sustain communities.

The Province may operate under measurable commitments such as:

- annual or seasonal emissions ceilings;
- minimum river flows;
- water-withdrawal limits;
- watershed nutrient or contamination limits;
- soil-restoration requirements;
- air-quality floors;
- habitat or forestry protections.

These constraints can conflict with other real goods.

A winter emergency may make an old thermal plant attractive. Increased irrigation may protect the harvest. Rapid mine development may protect future industrial viability.

The Ecologist's role is not to represent purity against production. Their job is to ensure that the Province does not solve today's viability problem by silently destroying tomorrow's environment.

That makes ecological commitments part of S5's hardest questions.

## 11. Visual and material language

### Strong lean: revolutionary industrial solarpunk

The visual world should show **transition and reuse**, not clean futurism.

Likely images include:

- newly painted cooperative signs on factories carrying older corporate or state architecture;
- electrified rail under patched signaling infrastructure;
- modern wind and solar beside old hydro and reluctantly retained thermal plant;
- workshops fabricating replacement parts that once arrived by truck from abroad;
- old control panels beside improvised modern displays;
- fiber trays running through concrete utility tunnels built decades earlier;
- hand-labelled repairs, field cabinets, test equipment, patch panels, and notebooks;
- community assemblies held in repurposed administrative buildings;
- gardens and public space around infrastructure that is visibly maintained rather than hidden;
- rail stations and technical institutes functioning as civic spaces;
- machine halls, wetlands, farms, substations, clinics, depots, and workshops sharing the same landscape.

Maintenance scars are part of the beauty.

The reader should feel both:

> **Look what they inherited.**

and:

> **Look what they are learning to make their own.**

## 12. Tone consequences

### Decided: independence is hopeful, not a prelude to inevitable collapse

The country is under pressure, but the novel should not treat its political experiment as doomed by authorial fiat.

People experience ordinary joys. They are proud of things they have kept running. Worker-run facilities can genuinely succeed. Assemblies can solve problems. Technical people can enjoy their work. Institutions can learn. People can change their minds without humiliation.

The central emotional proposition remains Beer's hopeful one:

> **Coherence and freedom can be designed together.**

The country will make mistakes because it is inventing structures under real constraints. Those mistakes create the story; they are not proof that the independence project was foolish.

The larger arc is:

> **We won independence. Now we have to learn how to remain independent.**

## 13. What the world should not become

Avoid:

- a direct fictionalization of Rojava, Kurdistan, Chile, the USSR, or any one historical case;
- a generic failed-state narrative;
- a command economy with revolutionary flags pasted over it;
- a frictionless worker-cooperative utopia;
- a green future whose abundance eliminates hard tradeoffs;
- an AI-governed society;
- a world where advanced technology implies advanced institutional coordination;
- a world where technological backwardness is used to explain every difficulty;
- making departed foreign managers secretly indispensable to basic operations;
- making workers automatically competent at every metasystem problem because they understand their own plant;
- making central institutions inherently authoritarian or local institutions inherently virtuous;
- using the embargo as an excuse for cartoon geopolitical villains;
- worldbuilding exposition that arrives before a character needs the fact.

The world exists to make the distinction between **owning infrastructure, operating infrastructure, and organizing infrastructure** visible.

## 14. Open decisions

These remain deliberately unfixed:

1. **Country and Province names.**
2. **Exact route to independence.** Negotiated separation, collapse of a larger state, revolution, war, or hybrid remains open.
3. **Exact external pressure.** Oil embargo is the leading shape; responsible powers and geopolitical details remain open.
4. **Formal constitution.** Decentralized/confederal political texture is decided; the exact institutions and legal language are not.
5. **Degree of worker ownership versus public ownership.** Both exist; their boundaries should emerge from story needs.
6. **Exact geography.** Climate, coast, mountains, rivers, agricultural zones, and resources remain open, although winter retains strong dramatic value.
7. **How long ago independence occurred when the novel opens.** Recent enough that institutions are still forming; exact timing is open.
8. **The Old Cyberneticist's former institute and failed project.** Function is decided; fictional history is not.
9. **The generative machine's exact hardware/software history.** Its inherited/offline character is a strong lean, not final technical lore.
10. **Factorio naming/licensing.** Working diegetic choice; keep structurally renameable.
11. **Specific historical and architectural influences.** Use multiple sources rather than mapping the fictional country onto one real culture.

## 15. Scene test

When worldbuilding enters a scene, it should usually answer at least one of these questions:

- What did independence change about this facility or relationship?
- What capability was inherited, and what supporting institution disappeared?
- What does this character want?
- What can physically fail here?
- What knowledge is local, and what knowledge exists elsewhere?
- What information can this character actually see?
- Who has authority to act, and is that authority still being negotiated?
- What imported resource or missing supply chain constrains the choice?
- What ecological commitment constrains the choice?
- Who elsewhere pays for an emergency rescue?
- What does the measured quantity mean to a person who depends on it?
- Is the Coordinator looking for a broken component when the real problem is a broken relationship?

If a world detail answers none of them, it probably belongs in notes rather than the novel.

The standing rule remains:

> **Build only enough world to make the next human and technical decision real.**

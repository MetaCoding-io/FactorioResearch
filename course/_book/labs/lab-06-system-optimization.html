13  Lab 6 — System Optimization

Scenario: fp-06-system-optimization · runs of ~18 minutes each

Hands you’ll need

Everything from Labs 3–5; if it’s been a while, the Practice Range’s six drills are a five-minute refresher.

Every previous lab isolated one idea and handed you one dial. The capstone takes the dials away. The factory is squeezed from both ends at once — a supplier who won’t slow down and a customer who won’t wait — and the toolbox contains every remedy this course has taught: faster machines, buffer chests, circuit components. Most combinations of them fail. The lab is over when you can say why the ones that fail, fail — before running them.

13.1 The system

The familiar fast → slow → fast line (37.5 / 30 / 45 workpieces per minute), with two new external facts:

  • A scheduled supplier. 36 workpieces/min arrive on a fixed schedule, whether you’re ready or not, into a warehouse holding at most 100 with a 10-unit overflow yard behind it. Deliveries that find both full are lost forever — the supply-loss metric counts them against everything the supplier scheduled. The warehouse starts nearly full (90) so the line never starves at startup — but do not mistake the quiet opening minutes for health. An empty line swallows a lot of material while its belts and machine buffers fill, and that cold-start gulp drains the warehouse; the structural 6/min surplus then spends the first stretch of the run silently refilling it. The loss doesn’t start until the buffers are full again — and then it never stops.
  • A customer, as in Lab 5: 33/min demanded, 30 s patience, FIFO backlog, on-time item rate watching.

Arithmetic the learner can do at the whiteboard before touching anything: the line’s constraint produces 30/min. The supplier delivers 36. The customer wants 33. Every minute, 6 units pile up at the gate and 3 units of demand go unserved. Both ends bleed, from one cause.

13.2 The objectives — and why there is no score

Requirements:  on-time item rate >= 95%   AND   supply loss <= 2%
Preference:    minimize average WIP

The requirements are a conjunction: pass both or the run is infeasible. The preference only ranks runs within the feasible set — fisl compare prints an infeasible run’s WIP but marks it as not competing. There is no weighted score, deliberately: a score would let 7.33 units of WIP buy back a failed customer, and Lab 5 already showed where that leads. An incomplete measurement evaluates as UNDETERMINED — never quietly as a pass or a failure.

13.3 Run 1 — watch it fail twice

Run the baseline untouched and watch both requirements fail on one screen: the on-time rate sinking as backlog accumulates (it ends the window at 33.3% against the 95% requirement), and — later than you’d expect — the supply-loss row waking up, finishing at 6.1% against the 2% cap. Note when each failure becomes visible. The service failure shows within minutes; the supply failure hides for roughly ten minutes behind refilling buffers, then arrives all at once and permanently — the measured 6.1% is ~33 units lost at ~6/min over the window’s last stretch, exactly the warehouse arithmetic. A ten-minute audit of this factory would have reported one problem; the system has two. One more honest cell: the baseline’s p95 customer wait prints censored — enough demand was simply never served that the tail wait has no measurable value. Walk the line mid-run and collect the full diagnosis kit from earlier labs: which machine never rests (Lab 2, throughput pinned at 30/min), who is blocked and who is starved (Lab 4: 5.5% blocked above the constraint, 11.3% starved below), where the queue sits (Lab 3, average WIP 57), what the customer sees (Lab 5).

13.4 Run 2 — spend your effort

Two committed reference runs bracket the space; your own combinations are the real assignment.

  • A — buffer the inflow (the seductive partial fix): splice a chest into the belt upstream of the constraint. Supply loss stops — measured 0.0%, intake jumps to 37.47/min — and the service failure doesn’t move: 33.1% on time, indistinguishable from the baseline’s 33.3%, while average WIP nearly doubles to 102.4 (167 at the bell). One requirement green, one red: INFEASIBLE. You moved the loss from the supplier’s scrap heap into your own inventory account, at full carrying cost.
  • B — relieve the constraint: swap the middle machine for the 45/min model. The line’s pace moves to 37.5/min — above the supplier’s 36 and the customer’s 33 — and both requirements pass with one action, because both symptoms shared one cause: 100% on time, p95 wait 1.47 s, zero loss, throughput 37.53/min. Average WIP settles at 33.57 — lower than the untouched baseline’s, and the preference value the feasible set is ranked by.

13.5 Debrief

  • Solution A passed the requirement everyone upstream could see and failed the one the customer feels. Which dashboards in a real company report the first kind of number? Who reports the second?
  • The baseline’s supply loss was invisible for most of the run — buffer space absorbed a structural 6/min deficit until it didn’t. How long would a measurement window have to be before you’d trust a “no loss” reading? What plays the role of that buffer space in a real supply agreement?
  • Why did one machine swap fix two apparently unrelated problems? State the general rule about symptoms and constraints.
  • Now raise the supplier to 40/min (above M1’s 37.5 ceiling). No machine swap fixes intake. What are your options, and what does each cost in which currency — loss, inventory, or capacity? (This is the first scenario in the course with no clean answer. That’s the point.)
  • Across seven labs you measured stocks, flows, constraints, states, service, and loss — always with declared boundaries, windows, and denominators. Which of those habits survives contact with a real operation where nobody hands you a conservation ledger?

13.6 What the whole course assumed away

Determinism, to the end. Every rate in this factory is a constant, so every diagnosis is an arithmetic identity and every fix either works or doesn’t. Real systems add variability — and with it, buffers earn keep, utilization near 100% becomes dangerous rather than merely pointless, and “how much capacity is enough” becomes a distribution question. The variability course starts exactly where this lab’s 40/min homework leaves off: when the clean answer stops existing, what do you buy insurance with — inventory, capacity, or time?

Instructor notes
  • All numbers in this chapter are measured (reference dataset in course/data/lab-06-comparison.json): baseline INFEASIBLE on both requirements (33.3% on time, 6.1% loss, TH 30.00/min, WIP 57.04, p95 censored); a-buffer-the-inflow INFEASIBLE on service alone (33.1% on time, 0.0% loss, WIP 102.41 and climbing — final 167); b-upgrade-constraint feasible (100% on time, p95 1.47 s, 0.0% loss, TH 37.53/min, WIP 33.57 — the preference value).
  • Baseline mechanics (learned the hard way — a 10-minute window measured only 0.8% loss): the cold-start belt fill plus machine buffers drain the warehouse during warmup, so the measured window opens with tens of units of free warehouse space. The 6/min structural surplus spends ~12 minutes (from supply start) refilling that space before overflow begins; thereafter essentially the full 6/min is lost. The measured 6.1% over 15 minutes is ~33 of 540 scheduled units — the last ~5½ minutes of the window losing at the full deficit rate, exactly the whiteboard arithmetic.
  • The masking effect is part of the lesson (see Run 1 and the debrief): transient buffer space hides a structural deficit; short audits report health. The baseline’s censored p95 is a second teachable: with enough never-served demand, the tail wait honestly has no value.
  • The in-game panel shows all three objective rules from READY onward (visibility discloses them); verdicts appear only in the post-run report — no provisional in-game pass/fail.
  • The supply-loss denominator is the supplier’s scheduled quantity in the window (telemetry records both scheduled arrivals and overflow losses); the metric is a fraction with exact integers behind it.

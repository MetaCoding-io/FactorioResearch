11  Lab 5 — Push and Pull

Scenario: fp-05-push-and-pull · runs of ~13 minutes each

Every lab so far had a silent customer: the sink took whatever arrived, whenever it arrived. This lab gives the customer a voice — a deterministic demand of 12 workpieces/min with a declared patience of 30 seconds — and one number that speaks for them: the fraction of demanded work served within that patience, the on-time item rate. The factory is Lab 3’s line, capacity 15/min. It can serve this customer. The question is what else your control policy does while serving them — and what happens when a policy stops serving them.

11.1 What “95% service” means here, exactly

FISL refuses to print an ambiguous “service level.” The on-time item rate is defined so every part is auditable:

  • demand arrives in cohorts — the quantity created at each boundary tick, aged from that tick;
  • deliveries are matched to the oldest outstanding demand first (FIFO) — a fresh delivery can’t be declared on-time while older demand waits;
  • a demanded unit is on time if fulfilled within 30 s of its creation; passing the deadline fixes the outcome — later fulfillment still reduces backlog, but history is not rewritten;
  • the rate’s numerator and denominator refer to the same cohorts: demand created during the measured phase, followed to their deadlines through a short service_tail phase after production measurement ends. Demand created before the window (opening backlog) or after it never touches the ratio;
  • a deadline the experiment didn’t live to observe is censored — reported as unresolved, never guessed on-time or late.

You get a second customer number: the p95 wait — the wait below which 95% of demanded quantity was served. If any selected demand was never served within the horizon, this comes back censored rather than as a number, because an unserved unit’s wait is not zero, not infinite, and not knowable.

11.2 Run 1 — push

Run the line untouched: admission at full inserter speed, production at 15/min against demand of 12/min. Predict: on-time rate? p95 wait? And what does the factory look like? (You did Lab 3. You know: the input belt packs solid, WIP in the dozens.) The customer, notice, is perfectly happy — overproduction does serve demand. It just pays for that service with inventory, flow time, and a stream of surplus the customer never asked for (surplus is recorded separately and never credits future demand).

11.3 Run 2 — pull, two ways

  • A — pull to consumption: Lab 3’s gate, verbatim (wire, relay hop, rough < 2 at the last belt before machine 1). Admission paces itself to the bottleneck (15/min ≥ 12/min demand). Measured: on-time 100.0% and p95 wait 3.02 s — identical to push, to the hundredth of a second — while average WIP fell 62.9% (51.70 → 19.16) and cycle time with it. Pull is free here; capacity covers demand, and push’s extra inventory was buying nothing.
  • B — the over-tight pull (deliberately wrong): a combinator clock admits one workpiece per 6 seconds — exactly 10.00/min measured, below the 12/min demand. Average WIP 7.33 — the smallest number in this course. And: on-time rate 22.5%, and the p95 wait comes back censored because some demand was simply never served. The 22.5% is arithmetic, not noise: backlog grows 2/min while service runs at 10/min, so a cohort’s wait exceeds 30 s once backlog passes 5 — about 2.5 minutes in. Only the first quarter of the measured demand was ever going to make it. Read that report slowly — it is what a “lean” factory that forgot its customer looks like in honest instrumentation.
fisl solutions scenarios/factory-physics/fp05-push-and-pull --run \
    --json course/data/lab-05-comparison.json

The measured three-run comparison is the lab:

admission avg WIP on-time p95 wait
push (baseline) 15.00/min 51.70 100.0% 3.02 s
A — pull-tuned 14.30/min 19.16 100.0% 3.02 s
B — over-tight 10.00/min 7.33 22.5% censored

Read column by column: B wins the WIP column decisively — and it is the only run that isn’t a factory. Push and A have identical customers and a 2.7× inventory difference. The whole lab is in that table.

11.4 Debrief

  • The canonical objective form here is a requirement plus a preference: maintain on-time ≥ 95% within 30 s; then minimize average WIP. Why must it be ordered like that, rather than a single blended score? What would a blended score have said about run B?
  • Solution A’s gate watches the bottleneck, not the customer. Why does that work here? Under what change (demand 16/min? machine slowdown?) would it stop working, and which metric would tell you first?
  • Backlog and on-time rate measure different things. Construct a run (mentally) with high on-time rate and repeated nonzero backlog — and one with the opposite. Which customer is angrier?
  • The censored p95 in run B is a feature. What would a dashboard that filled it in with “the waits we happened to observe” have concluded?

11.5 What the simulation assumed away

Demand here is a metronome. Real demand fluctuates — and the moment it does, the buffer question from Lab 4 and the service question from this lab collide: safety stock and safety capacity exist to protect service against variability. That collision is the variability course. What carries over unchanged: service is a constraint you state explicitly — with a tolerance, a cohort population, and an observation horizon — or it is a vibe.

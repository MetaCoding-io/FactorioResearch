Before the First Lab

This chapter is for the reader who has never played Factorio — or never run FISL. It contains everything the course assumes about your hands and your machine, and nothing else. If you already play Factorio and have run fisl run once, skip straight to Lab 0.

What you need

  • Factorio, the game (factorio.com or Steam, ~$35 — this course cannot be done with the free demo, and honesty obliges us to say that before you read three chapters). The course pins version 2.0.77; any machine that runs the game comfortably is enough, no expansion required.
  • Python 3.11 or newer and a terminal you’re comfortable pasting commands into.
  • About 30 minutes for setup, once.

Nothing else from Factorio’s world is required: no prior knowledge of its technology tree, no combat (our worlds have no enemies), no rocket. The course uses perhaps a dozen of the game’s hundreds of mechanics, and this chapter lists all of them.

One-time setup

git clone git@github.com:MetaCoding-io/FactorioResearch.git
cd FactorioResearch
pip install -e '.[dev]'     # the quotes matter in zsh

# tell fisl where Factorio lives (add to your shell profile):
export FACTORIO_BIN=/path/to/factorio/bin/x64/factorio

The FISL measurement mods never need manual installation — every run copies them into its own isolated workspace automatically.

How a lab session works

Every lab follows the same operational rhythm:

  1. In a terminal: fisl run scenarios/factory-physics/<lab>. This compiles the experiment, starts a private Factorio server from the lab’s pristine baseline world, and prints a port number.
  2. fisl run also prints a ready-to-paste client command (correct port and mod directory included) — copy it into a second terminal and the game opens straight into the lab.
  3. In-game you’ll spawn on the lab floor. The FISL panel (top left) shows READY. Walk around, look at everything, build if the lab calls for it — the experiment clock does not start until you press Start Experiment. The READY world is your practice range: nothing you do before Start is measured, and a fresh fisl run always begins from the same pristine world.
  4. Press Start Experiment and operate. Two rules are enforced, not suggested: there is no pause (the clock is simulation ticks), and disconnecting mid-run aborts the run — data preserved, run marked aborted. Aborting is cheap; just run again.
  5. When the final phase ends, the panel shows your results, and fisl report runs/<run_id> prints the full measurement record.

The controls — all of them

Factorio has hundreds of keybindings. The course uses these (defaults shown; Settings → Controls lists them all):

You want to Do
walk W A S D
open / close your inventory E (also closes most windows)
open a machine or chest left-click it
take items from a chest click items in its window (shift-click moves a stack)
place the item in your hand left-click the ground
rotate the item in your hand (or a placed entity under the cursor) R
empty your hand Q (over an entity, Q instead copies it into your hand — handy)
remove an entity hold right-click on it until it pops into your inventory
set a machine’s recipe open it, pick the recipe from its panel
see what everything is doing Alt — toggles the info overlay; the course’s figures assume it’s on
drag a circuit wire Alt+R (red) or Alt+G (green) puts the wire tool in your hand; click one entity, then the other
fullscreen Alt+Enter

Two mechanics deserve a sentence each, because labs from Lab 3 onward lean on them:

  • Inserters have a direction. An inserter grabs from the tile behind itself and drops in front; with the Alt overlay on, the arrow shows the drop direction. When a lab says “splice a chest into the belt,” that means: remove one belt tile, place the chest there, and place one inserter feeding the chest and one taking from it — arrows pointing along the original flow.
  • Wires have a reach (9 tiles). A wire that would stretch further simply doesn’t connect — the game won’t stop you from trying, and an unconnected control wire silently controls nothing. Check your connections visually. (This course’s own reference solutions got burned by exactly this; you will read that story in Lab 3.)

Reading the factory floor

The labs describe Factorio’s objects in plant-floor language. The dictionary, once:

In the game In the course Role
transport belt conveyor moves material; holds inventory in transit
fast inserter material handler moves items between neighbors; has direction
assembling machine 1/2/3 workstation (slow / medium / fast) transforms material by a recipe, at a fixed rate
wooden / steel chest buffer store holds inventory in place
blue source port receiving dock where material enters the system — the declared boundary
orange sink port shipping dock where finished goods leave — the other boundary
toolbox chest (near spawn) maintenance crib the parts you’re allowed to redesign with; never part of any measurement
constant / decider combinator controller (PLC) reads signals, applies a rule, drives equipment
substation, solar panel power distribution keep everything energized; in these labs you can ignore them

The two ports are FISL bench equipment: indestructible, not openable, not yours to move. They are the system boundary you’ll read about in every lab.

Which labs need which hands

  • Labs 0–2: walking and watching. Genuinely none of the building skills — your first three labs are safe territory to learn the controls in.
  • Labs 3–4: placing and removing entities, the chest splice, a machine swap, and your first circuit wire.
  • Labs 5–6: combinators and enable conditions — the deepest game skill the course uses, introduced by the lab chapters when needed.

If you’re nervous about the hands: the next chapter, The Practice Range, is a dedicated sandbox with six graded drills covering every manipulation above — and any lab’s READY floor also works as free practice space that resets completely on the next run.
